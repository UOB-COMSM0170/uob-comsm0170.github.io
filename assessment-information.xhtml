Overview

The goal of each coursework is to give you experience of applying machine learning tools to real data.

The intended learning outcomes for both pieces of coursework are the same as those for the unit as a whole:

  • Apply foundational AI techniques to analyse and solve typical business/healthcare problems;

  • Write and interpret Python code used to build and evaluate machine learning models;

  • Assess the risks, limitations, and appropriate use cases of AI in business and healthcare applications;

  • Communicate AI concepts and logic clearly to both technical and non-technical audiences.

Your tasks in the Group Coursework and in the Individual Coursework are:

  • Explore how machine learning methods can be used to extract useful findings from a real-world data set.

  • Develop models, analyses, and visualisations that tell a story about the data.

  • Critically evaluate the work to articulate its strengths and, crucially, weaknesses.

  • Submit a clear and useful report describing your work and your findings.

  • Write a thoughtful and interesting reflective discussion of the project.

  • Submit a carefully written and structured Python notebook.

  • Submit a clear and informative code review that demonstrates your understanding of how your code works.

  • Work effectively as part of a group (only applied to the Group Coursework).

For the Group Coursework you will work in a group of 5 or 6 students. All students in your group will be on the same MSc programme and will work together on the same dataset. If you would like to work with specific people, please send a message to the Unit Director, cc:ing all the people that want to work together.

The Individual Coursework will build on the work that you carry out for the Group Coursework, so it is important to be fully engaged with the Group Coursework.

Group Coursework Brief, Guidance & FAQs

We recommend reading this entire section in detail before starting the Group Coursework.

Group Coursework Deliverables

The Group Coursework has three deliverables:

  1. Your Report: Your 8-page Report will be one PDF that combines:
    • Group Report (pages 1 to 6)
    • References + Collaboration Statement + AI Statement (page 7)
    • Individual Reflective Discussion (page 8)

    Pages 1 to 7 should be identical for every member of your group; page 8 is your own individual work.

  2. Your Python Notebook: Submit the single notebook containing all code used by your group. Every group member submits the same notebook.
  3. Your Code Review: Prepare and submit your own short, independent code-review video.

Note: Please adhere to the 8-page limit for the report. If there are any additional pieces of work or sets of results that could not be included, including tables, visualizations, animations, etc., these could be hosted on a publicly accessible GitHub page for the project – but do ensure that the submitted Report pdf can be understood and marked as a self-contained document.

Group Report

All members of the group will submit the same Group Report and will be given the same mark for this component of the project. It is up to each team to decide their best strategy to tackle this coursework, i.e., how to divide the tasks and how to work together.

The Group Report should be written and structured like an academic report with a title, list of authors, abstract, introduction, and sections reporting your data preparation, data modelling, findings, a discussion and a conclusion.

  • Abstract: A succinct account of the project and its findings.

  • Introduction: The background context for the problem tackled by the project.

  • Data Preparation and Initial Exploration: What data manipulation was necessary to prepare the dataset. What was learned from an initial exploration of the data.

  • Data Modelling: Details of the models, visualisations, and analyses that you developed, their strengths and weaknesses, and justifications for your approach and choices. You should include a brief technical description of any algorithms, tools, and methods used (referencing the scientific literature or equivalent wherever appropriate).

  • Findings: Details of your findings, results, insights, etc. Use appropriate visualisations.

  • Discussion and Conclusion: What has the project delivered in terms of knowledge, understanding and insight into the problem that it addressed?

  • References: The above sections should all fit within the first six pages. A seventh page can be used to list any references cited in the report.

  • Collaboration Statement: The seventh page should also include your Collaboration Statement signed by all members of the group stating that all members contributed roughly equally.

  • AI Statement: The seventh page should also include your AI Statement. A paragraph detailing how any AI tools were used during the project, including example prompts.

The marking criteria for the Group Report are:

  • Problem Understanding: To what extent have you understood and explained the goals of the project, taking account of end-user requirements (15 marks).

  • Data Wrangling and Exploration: To what extent has extraction, preparation and manipulation of the data been robust, systematic and sophisticated; Depth of insights gained through the initial exploration of the data (15 marks).

  • Data Modelling, Visualisation, Analysis and Evaluation: Effectiveness of tools chosen and deployed; Appropriateness of the modelling approach; Soundness of machine learning methods applied; Sophistication of the visualisations employed; Effectiveness and insight of the evaluation (40 marks).

  • Critical Evaluation, Discussion and Conclusion: Consideration of the project’s strengths and weaknesses; Utility of the project findings for the end user (15 marks).

  • Presentation: Fluency and coherence of the written text; Quality of the figures, charts, graphs, etc. (15 marks).

Reflective Discussion

You will include your own one-page individual reflective discussion of your work as the final page of your report (page 8). This will describe your role in the group, what your achievements were, and what you have learned from your involvement in the project. The quality of this piece of work, combined with your mentor’s assessment of your contribution to the project will determine the final mark that you receive.

Each group’s report will be marked using the criteria described above and this will be used to set an overall mark for each group. Each individual’s mark will be determined based on this overall mark (so it is in everyone’s interests to collaborate well) with a potential bonus based on the individual’s contribution. This will be assessed partly by your attendance at and contribution to discussion with your group’s mentor and partly based on the 1-page reflective discussion document that describes your role in the group, what your achievements were, and what you have learned from the course.

Your reflective discussion could contain the following kinds of material, but the content is up to you:

  • Account of your personal contribution to the project.

  • Discussion of the overall strengths and weaknesses of the project.

  • Reflection on how the project related to the ideas taught on the unit.

  • Discussion of your achievements and what the project experience taught you.

The (equally weighted) marking criteria for the reflective discussion are:

  • Significance of your contribution to the project.

  • Appreciation of the project’s strengths and weaknesses.

  • Appreciation of how the material taught on the unit relates to the project.

  • Reflection on achievements and what was learned from the project experience.

Note: This reflective discussion can only increase an individual’s mark above that achieved by for the Group Report. However, in exceptional scenarios such as a group losing an individual or an individual not contributing to the group work, their mentor’s assessment will determine the final mark each individual receives.

Python Notebook

You will submit your group’s code in the form of a single Python notebook. This notebook should be runnable by the person marking your work. The code should be well-organised and clear. Any issues with the code (e.g., parts that do not completely work, or are unfinished) should be noted in comments.

Code Review

Your code review presentation should be a video no longer than 5 mins. On the screen you will show your code – you will scroll through the code explaining key features and demonstrating your understanding of how the code works. A typical code review presentation might include the following:

  • Introduction: A few seconds introducing the problem tackled by the project.

  • Overall Approach: A few seconds overviewing the approach that you took.

  • Code Walkthrough: A few minutes walking through the code, explaining how it works.

  • Deep Dive: A few minutes focussing on a part of the code that you were most responsible for developing.

  • Issues: A few seconds on any remaining challenges, problems or open issues that require further work.

  • Conclusion: A few seconds concluding the video.

The (equally weighted) marking criteria for the presentation are:

  • Clarity: The descriptions and explanations are clear and compelling.

  • Technical Command: Treatment of technical issues is detailed, deep and convincing.

  • Effective Teamwork: Good evidence of collaboration, planning, communication.

  • Good Presentation: Presentation is succinct, clear, well-structured, comprehensive.

Note: the presentation itself need not be particularly sophisticated. An efficient, clear and informative presentation is what you should be aiming for, not one that is fancy.

Note: the point of this component is to enable us to get a good sense of how much you understand about how the group’s code works – so general or vague descriptions will not be sufficient – but, on the other hand, there will be parts of the code that were written by other students, so you are not expected to be an expert on every line of code. Also, most groups will have tried a few things, and some will not have worked perfectly, or some will not have worked at all – giving an honest account of the successes and failures is better than just trying to tell a positive but partial story.

Note: one easy way to make a video could be to join a Teams meeting, hit record, share your screen so that your code is visible (make sure the font is large enough to read), and spend five minutes describing the code. At the end of the meeting, Teams should let you download the video. Then you can crop off the beginning and the end to produce a simple 5-min.

Important General Marking Guidelines and FAQs

Note: Reports will be checked for plagiarism and academic misconduct. Proven plagiarism will result in a coursework grade of 0 for the individual student or group of students that submitted the report. Your use of AI during coursework and report writing should follow University and Faculty Guidelines in which all prompts must be cited. Bear in mind that ChatGPT and other language models struggle to write academic reports to a high standard of 2.1 or above. Hallucinated text will penalise you heavily as it shows a lack of understanding. Note that not referencing the prompts you use represents academic misconduct and poor academic practice!

Note: Where a student is judged to be at a grade boundary (e.g., between a 2:1 and a 1st-class mark), they may be required to attend a short viva which will influence their final mark.

FAQs:

Q1. How will individual marks be assigned to each member in the Group Coursework?

All members in the group will receive the same marks for the final report. Reflective discussion may increase the mark that an individual receives. In exceptional scenarios, however, the coursework mentor’s assessment will determine the final mark that group member receives.

Q2. What if one or more members of my group doesn’t engage with the coursework?

We expect each member of the group to contribute equally (though this could be in different ways – maybe one student is working on the data preparation and report writing, another on data exploration and visualisation, another researching and developing various models). If this is not the case, you should contact the unit director ASAP. By submitting the report with a signed collaboration statement, it is assumed that all group members are happy with the contribution of their team members.

Q3. What if one or more of my team members becomes ill or has exceptional circumstances and has to resit?

In this case it is expected that the remaining members of the group will finish and complete the coursework on their own. Further, each member of the group should highlight in their individual reflection discussion how they navigated such circumstances. This will be taken into consideration during marking.

Q4. What is the signed collaboration agreement?

You must include the following on page seven of your report: “We agree that all members have contributed to this project in an approximately equal manner” with signatures of all group members below it (a list of names is sufficient if digital signatures are hard to secure).

Q5. How should the report be formatted?

Please use a format that matches the example reports linked below – this is designed to allow you to fit quite a lot of material on one page without resorting to tiny font sizes, etc. The IEEE conference format (https://www.overleaf.com/latex/templates/ieee-conference-template/grfzhhncsfqn) was used for the example reports. If you are happy to use LaTeX, you could use this same format – but you are not required to do this.

Q6. I have a Study Support Plan (SSP).

Talk to the Unit Director as early as possible and we can discuss how we can tailor the coursework to your SSP.

Q7. I am worried about the level of work that I am expected to deliver for this unit.

We fully appreciate that many of the students on this unit may not have had much experience of programming, AI, machine learning, etc. Our approach to marking both pieces of coursework will take this into account!
